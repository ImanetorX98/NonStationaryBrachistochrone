        Abstract. What is the fastest constrained worldline in a spacetime that is
        itself evolving? In a stationary spacetime the relativistic brachistochrone reduces
        to Fermat/Randers optics with a conserved rail energy supplied by a timelike
        Killing vector; in a dynamical spacetime no such conservation law exists and the
        variational problem becomes non-autonomous. We address this by formulating the
        brachistochrone as a controlled-rail optimal-control problem, in which the invariant
        −u · W = Ê is actively maintained along the worldline by a selector W that follows a
        Killing → conformal-Killing → Kodama hierarchy and reduces to the Kodama vector
        when no timelike Killing field survives. We prove that this construction is a legitimate
        Pontryagin problem—with existence, normality, and a non-autonomous Hamilton–
        Jacobi verification criterion in place of the stationary conservation law—and then
        solve two spherically symmetric non-stationary cases in closed form: spatially flat
        Friedmann–Lemaı̂tre–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) and the ingoing Vaidya dynamical
        black hole. For Vaidya we obtain the plunge phenomenology and a first-order adiabatic
        correction in closed special-function form, verified against the true non-autonomous
        flow; the construction cleanly separates the effects of spatial curvature from those
        of radial mass flow. The rotating, axisymmetric conformal-Kerr (Thakurta–Kerr)
        application is developed in a companion paper.

1. Introduction

The brachistochrone—the path of fastest descent—is a classical variational problem
whose relativistic analogue asks for the fastest constrained “rail” worldline from a launch
event to a fixed spatial target, at a free value of the arrival clock. In a stationary
spacetime the answer is well understood: a timelike Killing vector supplies a conserved
rail energy, and the problem reduces to Fermat’s principle and to optical (Randers–
Zermelo–Finsler) geometry [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The physically central regimes of
gravitation, however—cosmological expansion, gravitational collapse, accretion—are not
stationary.
     When the geometry itself evolves, the ingredients that make the stationary problem
tractable disappear: there is no timelike Killing field, hence no conserved energy to label
the extremals, and the variational problem is genuinely non-autonomous. Whether a
well-posed fastest constrained worldline even exists in such spacetimes, what invariant
should replace the Killing energy, and how time dependence—as opposed to spatial
curvature—reshapes the optimal path, have not been addressed. This is the question
the present paper answers.
     Our starting point is that the rail energy need not be a symmetry charge: it can
be an invariant that is actively maintained along the worldline. We formulate the
brachistochrone as a controlled-rail optimal-control problem in which −u · W = Ê is
held fixed by a selector W following a Killing → conformal-Killing → Kodama hierarchy;
in a dynamical spherically symmetric spacetime W is the Kodama vector, whose charge
is conserved along the controlled worldline even where free fall is not. Pontryagin’s
maximum principle makes this a legitimate time-optimal control problem, supplying
existence, normality and a non-autonomous Hamilton–Jacobi verification criterion in
place of the stationary conservation law.
     We develop this general framework in section 2 and then apply it to two spherically
symmetric non-stationary spacetimes: the spatially flat FLRW base (section 3) and
the ingoing Vaidya dynamical black hole (section 4). For Vaidya we obtain the plunge
phenomenology and a first-order adiabatic correction in closed special-function form,
verified against the true non-autonomous flow; the construction cleanly separates the
effects of spatial curvature from those of radial mass flow. The rotating, axisymmetric
conformal-Kerr (Thakurta–Kerr) case—frame dragging, an ergosphere and genus-two
closed forms—is developed in the companion Paper II [8], which cites the foundations
proved here.

coordinate change alone (e.g. to Doran variables) does not alter the geometric selector
and hence cannot substitute for it.

The optimal-control problem. Precisely, the brachistochrone is the following time-
optimal control problem. The state is the spatial position x = (r, ϕ) carried along
the worldline; the evolution parameter is the selected clock s ∈ {t, τ, η, v}; the control
is the unit spatial direction θ ∈ S 1 of the velocity on the rail indicatrix (2), with
dynamics dx/ds = ẋ(θ; x, s) read off that indicatrix. The boundary data are a fixed
initial event (s0 , x0 ) and a fixed spatial target xf (a point, or a target set/worldline),
with the arrival clock sf left free; the cost is the elapsed clock sf − s0 . Because sf is
free, the Pontryagin transversality condition is H|s=sf = 0 [equation (3)]—a free final-
clock condition, not a two-event condition. We accordingly reserve “fixed endpoints” for
the two-point boundary-value problem of the companion paper, in which both spatial
endpoint positions are prescribed while the branch-dependent arrival clock is determined
by the optimal solution and remains the quantity being compared; the default problem
fixes the spatial target at a free arrival clock.

Theorem I.1 (controlled-rail reduction). Minimizing travel time subject to (2) is a time-
optimal control problem with control θ. The velocity indicatrix (2) is a compact, strictly
convex closed curve (the boundary of a convex region), and the control is its direction
θ ∈ S 1 , itself a compact one-dimensional set. Maximizing the support function p · ẋ(θ)
over this curve has a unique maximizer by strict convexity, so no Filippov relaxation
is needed—the control set S 1 is already compact and the maximizer single-valued; we
do not claim the velocity locus is a convex subset of the plane, nor convexify it (which
would admit averaged velocities violating the unit-mass shell). Excluding p = 0 by
the nontriviality condition, the optimal control θ? (x, p) is single-valued and smooth
(away from the freezing and horizon degeneracies, treated separately) and Pontryagin’s
maximum principle [9, 10] delivers the branch Hamiltonian H(x, p) = maxθ p · ẋ(θ) in
closed form (section 4 (and the companion paper for Thakurta–Kerr)). This is a genuine
time-optimal control problem, for which the Pontryagin (vakonomic-type) reduction
is the correct prescription; we do not invoke the nonholonomic d’Alembert principle,

of H reproduce the Randers/Zermelo structure [6, 11].

Operational reading: thrust. Maintaining the controlled invariant generally requires
nonzero proper acceleration aµ = Duµ /dτ (a = 0 only on a free-fall geodesic), so the
rail force is the thrust per unit mass, spacelike (a · u = 0) with |a| diverging as the
freezing/horizon surface is approached. The present variational problem minimizes the
selected elapsed clock, not thrust, fuel or integrated control effort, which define distinct
optimal-control problems. In the non-stationary case the a · W term is the proper
acceleration that maintains the rail against the non-stationary mass-rate drift.

Geometric dictionary. The indicatrix (2) carries two independent data—its center
c = (cr , cϕ ) (the Zermelo wind) and the eccentricity of its semi-axes (the anisotropy of
the local speed, ar 6= aϕ )—and each governs a distinct feature of the brachistochrones,

  • eccentricity 6= 0 (a spatial gradient ∂r f 6= 0) ⇒ the t and τ branches split in depth,

Eccentricity and center offset are separate levers: the first switches on the t/τ split,
the second the inversions. In particular the three branches degenerate—the t, τ and η
brachistochrones coincide—iff the indicatrix is, up to a position-independent conformal
scale, a rigid centered circle (c = 0, ar = aϕ , radius depending on the flow parameter
alone), equivalently iff the optical cost ratios nt /nτ , nt /nη are position-independent.
This is exactly spatial homogeneity: FLRW is the unique such case in our ladder
(section 3), whereas any gradient or wind breaks it—even the static, non-rotating
Schwarzschild ellipse, which is centered (c = 0) yet eccentric, already splits t from
τ . The indicatrix shape is thus the organizing thread of the paper: FLRW (circle)
→ Vaidya (radial wind) → Thakurta–Kerr (angular wind) is a progression of ellipse
deformations, each switching on one further phenomenon.

2.2. Existence, normality, and global optimality
The Pontryagin reduction above supplies only necessary conditions. We record here
the three complementary facts that make the constrained problem well posed for the
non-stationary rail: an existence theorem for the minimizer, the normality of every
regular extremal (so the reduction −u · W = Ê is never vacuous), and a Hamilton–
Jacobi certificate that upgrades a saturated extremal to a global optimum. All three are
elementary under the standing hypothesis that the indicatrix is a compact, uniformly
convex oval [equation (2)].

Conjugate points, Maxwell set, cut locus. Two mechanisms end optimality. A conjugate
point (Jacobi) ends local minimality when the second variation degenerates; up to the
first conjugate point the extremal is a strict local minimum. A Maxwell point—two
distinct extremals of equal arrival time—ends global minimality earlier and is exactly a
shock (gradient discontinuity) of V , i.e. a point of the cut locus. Strict convexity of the

indicatrix removes Filippov relaxation but does not exclude Maxwell points: distinct
windings past the rotation axis, or the two ±J arcs reaching a common target, can
tie. A closed-form extremal admitting a single-valued saturated verification function
is globally certified only up to its first Maxwell or conjugate obstruction—the first
such singularity of V . For the generic non-stationary trajectories treated here we
establish PMP extremality and local numerical minimality checks, not a complete global
Hamilton–Jacobi certificate; beyond the first obstruction the global optimum is selected
by comparing arrival times across the competing extremals.

What is not claimed inside the ergosphere. Where the Killing selector ξ becomes
spacelike—inside the ergosphere—the invariant −u · ξ = Ê no longer bounds a timelike-

be required to formulate a compact future-timelike indicatrix, which we do not develop.
Freezing (where ρ → 0), turning (where a semi-axis vanishes), and the separatrix (a
double root of the radical) are distinct degeneracies and are not conflated.

4.2. Phenomenology: penetration, timing, bounce
Three features distinguish the dynamical hole from its frozen-mass Schwarzschild limit.
    Penetration. The τ -brachistochrone reaches the apparent horizon r = 2m(v)
only if |J| lies below a threshold Jc (v0 ); accretion (m0 > 0) opens a finite window
Jc > 0, whereas the negative-rate continuation (m0 < 0 in the ingoing metric—a formal
continuation; physical evaporation is the outgoing metric, section 4.3) closes it to zero
measure—an accretion/(negative-rate) asymmetry with no static analogue. The finite
Controlled-rail brachistochrones in non-stationary spacetimes                               15

capture interval J ∈ (0, Jc ] is the analogue of the Kerr t-branch dichotomy, generated
here by the dynamics: grazing orbits linger near periapsis while the growing horizon
closes the gap.
     Bounce.       The r-parametrization degenerates at periapsis (dv/dr → ∞);
reparametrizing by v makes the Euler–Lagrange problem regular at the turning point
r0 = 0, so the trajectory is integrated straight through the bounce—the same extremal,
via the exact change of variable (f v 0 −1)dr = (f −r0 )dv. The v-flow reaches the periapsis
exactly where the r-flow diverges (rmin = 3.0105 at v = 7.29 for µ ≡ m0 = 0.01), and
the endpoint-sensitive memory pv (r) ∝ m0 measures the departure from the frozen-mass
Schwarzschild curve (figure 8).
     Timing and the orbit–horizon race. Because m grows along the path, rmin depends
on when the particle arrives, the epoch v1 —a genuinely non-stationary effect absent in
Kerr. The absolute and relative penetration have opposite winners (figure 9): under the
negative-rate branch (m0 < 0) the absolute periapsis descends but the horizon 2m(v)
retreats faster, so the relative penetration rmin /2m(vperi ) worsens for later arrivals; under
accretion the periapsis rises yet the horizon grows faster, so late arrivals graze the relative
horizon. The orbit–horizon chase has reversed outcomes measured in r versus in horizon
units.

Proved. The non-stationary brachistochrone is a well-posed controlled-rail problem:
the invariant −u · W = Ê is legitimate via Pontryagin’s principle, with W fixed
by the Killing–CKV–Kodama hierarchy and the Kodama energy conserved along the
Vaidya rail even when free fall does not. Existence, normality, and the non-autonomous
Hamilton–Jacobi verification criterion hold on the timelike-selector domain. FLRW
is the degenerate base—freezing without branch splitting—while in Vaidya the mass
breaks the t/τ symmetry through its radial gradient.
