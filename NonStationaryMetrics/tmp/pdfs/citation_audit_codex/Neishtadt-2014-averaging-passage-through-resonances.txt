      Averaging, passage through resonances, and capture
           into resonance in two-frequency systems

                                     A. I. Neishtadt


       Abstract. Applying small perturbations to an integrable system leads to
       its slow evolution. For an approximate description of this evolution the
       classical averaging method prescribes averaging the rate of evolution over
       all the phases of the unperturbed motion. This simple recipe does not
       always produce correct results, because of resonances arising in the pro-
       cess of evolution. The phenomenon of capture into resonance consists in
       the system starting to evolve in such a way as to preserve the resonance
       property once it has arisen. This paper is concerned with application of
       the averaging method to a description of evolution in two-frequency sys-
       tems. It is assumed that the trajectories of the averaged system intersect
       transversally the level surfaces of the frequency ratio and that certain other
       conditions of general position are satisfied. The rate of evolution is charac-
       terized by a small parameter ε. The main content of the paper is a proof
       of the following result: outside a set of initial data with measure of order
       √                                                                  √
         ε the averaging method describes the evolution to within O( ε | log ε|)
       for periods of time of order 1/ε. This estimate is sharp. The exceptional
                        √
       set of measure ε contains the initial data for phase points captured into
       resonance. A description of the motion of such phase points is given, along
       with a survey of related results on averaging. Examples of capture into
       resonance are presented for some problems in the dynamics of charged par-
       ticles. Several open problems are stated.

                                      1. Introduction
   Applying small perturbations to an integrable system results in an evolution of
quantities which are first integrals of the unperturbed system. The classical averag-
ing method going back to Lagrange, Laplace, and Gauss gives a prescription for an
approximate description of this evolution. In the phase space we consider a region
which is foliated by invariant tori of the unperturbed system, with unperturbed
trajectories forming quasi-periodic windings on them. The averaging method pre-
scribes that the rate of evolution must be averaged over an invariant torus of the
unperturbed system. This produces a closed system which involves only slowly
varying variables for the description of the evolution. However, this simple and effi-
cient recipe does not always yield a correct result, because of resonances between
frequencies of the unperturbed motion which can arise in the process of evolution.
Some trajectories may behave very differently from what the averaging method
prescribes. The minimal characteristic size of an interval of time on which it is
reasonable to ask about the accuracy of the averaging method is inversely propor-
tional to the magnitude of the perturbation. Under fairly general assumptions the
set of initial data for which the averaging method fails to describe the dynamics on
such intervals of time has measure tending to zero together with the magnitude of
the perturbation (as a consequence of Anosov’s very general result [1]). Important
questions then arise on an estimate for the measure of this exceptional set, on the
fate of trajectories with initial data in this set, and of course on the accuracy of
the averaging method for initial data lying outside this set. These questions are
treated in the present survey for the case of two-frequency systems.
   In many cases the equations of motion of a system which differs from an inte-
grable system by a small perturbation can be reduced to the form

                       I˙ = εf (I, ϕ, ε),      ϕ̇ = ω(I) + εg(I, ϕ, ε),                     (1.1)

components ϕi of ϕ, and a dot over a symbol denotes the derivative with respect to

   The main feature of a general multifrequency system is as follows. In the course
of evolution the frequencies vary slowly and may become commensurable at some
moments of time (resonances can occur). For some relations between the phases,
capture into resonance can occur: the phase point starts to move in a way so as
to preserve the commensurability that has arisen. Averaging over all the phases
cannot be used to describe such a motion: the points I(t) and I(t) can differ even
by a quantity of order 1. The phenomenon of capture into resonance was first
considered in [4] and [5], in connection with some problems in celestial mechanics.
   Each resonance condition describes a surface called a resonant surface in the space
of slow variables. There are infinitely many resonances, and generically the points
on resonant surfaces form a dense set. However, the set has measure zero, and
this ensures that the averaging method is applicable. What mainly distinguishes
a two-frequency system (m = 2) from a general multifrequency system is a much
simpler configuration of the resonant surfaces in the space of slow variables (see
the discussion in [6]). The question of averaging in two-frequency systems which
pass through resonances in their evolution was first raised by Dirac [7] in connec-
tion with the problem of adiabatic invariants of two-frequency systems. In [7] he
considered a Hamiltonian system with two degrees of freedom which depends on
a parameter varying slowly with time. For each fixed value of the parameter the
system is completely integrable, and action-angle variables K, ϕ can be introduced
in it. The equations for the evolution of these variables in the problem with slowly
varying parameter reduce to the form (1.1), with the ‘actions’ K and the slow time
τ = εt as slow variables. The corresponding averaged system has the form K̇ = 0,
τ̇ = ε, so the evolution of the ‘action’ variables is absent in the approximation
of the averaging method (this was shown by Burgers [8]). However, even in this
approximation the frequencies do vary (because τ varies), and the system passes
through resonances. In [7] the problem was considered under the assumption that
the ratio of the frequencies varies at a non-zero rate along solutions of the exact
system. This prevents capture into resonance. It was shown that the ‘action’ vari-
ables are adiabatic invariants, that is, approximate first integrals of the original
system: their variation on an interval of time of length 1/ε tends to zero as ε → 0.
   A general two-frequency system (1.1) was considered by Arnold [9] under the
assumption (Condition A) that the frequency ratio varies at a non-zero rate along
solutions of the exact system (1.1). Condition A precludes resonance  √ sticking. It
was shown in [9] that, under this condition, ∥I(t) − I(t)∥ = O( ε | log ε|2 ) for
0 ⩽√ t ⩽ 1/ε and for all initial data (later in [10] the bound was improved to
O( ε )). Also, [9] pointed out the problem of finding estimates under Condition Ā,
which states that the frequency ratio varies at a non-zero rate along solutions of
the averaged system (1.2) (Condition Ā is clearly weaker than Condition A).

   In this paper we consider a two-frequency system satisfying Condition Ā along
with a certain condition of general position which we call Condition B, to be
explained below. In this case the picture of the motion is as follows. Capture can
only occur for finitely many resonances in the infinite set of existing resonances.
These are resonances in which the frequencies are commensurable at a sufficiently
low order. In a neighbourhood of each of these resonances the evolution of the reso-
nant combination of phases can be described by a second-order differential equation
which has the type of the equation of a pendulum with a constant external torque
and subject to small perturbations. This reduction of the problem was used by
several authors (in particular, see [5], [10]–[15]) and is currently standard (see,
for instance, [6], [16], [17]). Condition B means that the unstable singular points
of the corresponding unperturbed equation are non-degenerate. The evolution in
a neighbourhood of a resonance is determined by the character of motion of the
‘pendulum’. Under perturbations it can go from a rotation regime to an oscillation
regime, and this will mean capture into resonance. However, the   √ measure of the
initial data corresponding to such solutions becomes small (∼ ε ) as the pertur-
bation becomes small. Capture into resonance cannot occur for most initial data:
the point I(t) traverses the resonance zone rapidly enough that the resonance does
not have time to influence the motion significantly. The other resonance zones
(corresponding to higher-order commensurability) are rapidly traversed for all the
initial data. In non-resonant zones we can (as done in [9]) investigate the motion
by making a change of variables reducing (1.1) to (1.2) in first approximation.
   Estimates based on the picture of motion described above have led us to the
following
      √ central result: with the exception of a set of initial data with measure
κ & ε, the deviation of the slow component of the exact solution from the solution
of the averaged system for a period of time 1/ε has an upper bound of order
√
  ε | log κ|, and we give examples
                                 √ when this bound is attained. Furthermore, the
exceptional set of measure ∼ ε cannot be eliminated, since there are examples
when just this proportion √ of solutions is captured into resonance.
   This result (for κ ∼ ε) together with a sketch of its proof was presented in our
paper [10] and then reproduced in the books [6], [16], and [18]. However, no full
proof has been published. In this paper we present a full proof (leaving out the
proofs of several simple auxiliary results for lack of space). The exposition is based
on Chap. 2 in the author’s Ph.D. thesis [19]. We supplement the results in [19] by
a description of the motion of phase points captured into resonance, examples of
physical problems with capture into resonance, and a survey of related results. We
also state some open problems.
   The material in this paper is organized as follows. In § 2 we indicate conditions
which the system (1.1) under consideration must satisfy; in § 3 we state the main
theorem; § 4 is devoted to a discussion of these conditions and the main result. We

4.2. Remark about capture into resonance. The notion of capture into reso-
nance arises from looking at examples. In the general case its definition is imprecise
and relates to the situation when for some initial data the solution of (1.1) ‘sticks’ in
a neighbourhood of a resonant surface s1 ω1 + s2 ω2 = 0 for a period of time ∼ ε−1
and thus deviates strongly from the corresponding solution of (1.2). For all the
ambiguity of this description, it lets us assert that solutions with initial data in
the set V ′ introduced in Corollary 3.1 cannot be captured into resonance. Indeed,
for these solutions

the point I(t) also cannot stick in a neighbourhood of a resonant surface. So the
set of initial data for trajectories

therefore has measure O( ε ). The problem of finding such a bound was pointed
out in [21] (see also [22], problem 1972-9).
4.3. Discussion of Condition Ā. As noted above, Condition Ā ensures that
the frequency ratio ω1 /ω2 (if ω2 ̸= 0) or ω2 /ω1 (if ω1 ̸= 0) varies at a non-zero rate
along solutions of the averaged system (1.2), or equivalently, that trajectories of
the averaged system intersect the level surfaces of the frequency ratio transversally.
The usefulness of consideration of the problem under Condition Ā was indicated
in [9]. Condition A ensures that the frequency ratio varies at a non-zero rate along
solutions of the exact problem. Condition Ā is weaker than Condition A. The
relation (2.2) is obtained by averaging (3.1) over all ϕ1 and ϕ2 .

9.3. Classification of singular points and Condition B ′ . We say that a sin-
gular point has type a1 if there are separatrices going into it and going away to
infinity, and we say that a singular point has type a2 if it does not have type a1
but is joined to a point of type a1 by a separatrix or a sequence of separatrices. We
classify the other singular points as points of type a3.
   The phase portrait of equation (9.9) changes as the parameter x varies. As can
easily be verified, Condition B ensures that for x = x0 the singular points are
non-degenerate. Then such points exist and are non-degenerate also for x close
to x0 . We remark that Condition B can be relaxed slightly by assuming that only
points of types a1 and a2 are non-degenerate for x = x0 ; this does not change the
arguments that follow.
   If a point of type a2 is present, then the phase portrait is degenerate: there are
separatrices connecting saddle points. If a point has type a2 for x = x0 , then for
x arbitrarily close to x0 it can have type a1 or a3. If we have such a degeneracy
for x = x0 , then the arguments needed to prove Lemma 6.6 are significantly more
complicated. However, this degenerate situation is rare: Condition B ′ , which says
that separatrices do not connect saddle points for x = x0 , is a condition of general
position, like Condition B. In order not to overcomplicate the simple picture of the
non-degenerate case, we divide the rest of § 9 into sub-subsections 9.4.1 and 9.4.2.
In § 9.4.1 we prove Lemma 6.6 under Condition B ′ , and in § 9.4.2 we prove it
without this assumption (§ 9.4.2 is based on the results in § 9.4.1). If relaxing the
assumptions as much as possible is not all that important to the reader, then § 9.4.2
can be skipped.

                    13. Examples of capture into resonance
                      in the dynamics of charged particles
   The analysis of the motion of charged particles in a field is a constant source
of problems on averaging of perturbations. This tradition goes back to Alfvén’s
discovery of the adiabatic invariance of the magnetic moment of a particle moving
in a strong inhomogeneous magnetic field and his theory of guiding centre (see,
for instance, [40]). One comes to the theory of guiding centre by averaging with
respect to the fast rotation phase of the particle in a strong magnetic field [23], [41].
We consider the case when there is a constant background magnetic field and in
addition the electric field of a plasma wave acts on the particle. This is a classical
problem in plasma physics [40]. The regimes of motion of the particle can be
completely different here, depending on the relations between the parameters. We
shall look at the case of a high-frequency wave (that is, its frequency is much
larger than the angular velocity of rotation of the particle in the magnetic field)
with electric field of magnitude comparable with the background magnetic field.
The equations of motion, averaged with respect to the phase of the wave, describe
the slow rotation of the particle in the constant magnetic field. However, in this
rotation the projection of the velocity of the particle on the direction of wave
propagation varies, and a resonance can occur: the length of this projection becomes
comparable with the phase velocity of the wave (the so-called Cherenkov resonance).
Below we consider capture into such resonance for an (electrostatic) plasma wave
propagating orthogonally to the background magnetic field [42], [43]. The case of an
electrostatic wave propagating at some angle to the background magnetic field was
considered in [44] and [45] and the case of an electromagnetic wave in a plasma
was considered in [44], [46]–[50].

                                14. Open problems
   A) Problems on averaging in two-frequency systems.
   1) Tangency to a resonant surface. Above we considered two-frequency systems
under the assumption that the trajectories of the averaged system intersect all the
level surfaces of the frequency ratio transversally. However, if we look at sufficiently
long trajectories, we see that some of them can be tangent to these level surfaces
in the general case. Such trajectories are tangent or almost tangent to resonant
surfaces. No estimates for the accuracy of the averaging method are known in
this case. There are estimates in a model problem for a single-frequency system,
when trajectories of the averaged system are tangent to the surface on which the
frequency vanishes [29].
   2) Singularities of the foliation by tori in the unperturbed system. A system
with rotating phases of the form (1.1) describes the dynamics in a system which
is a small perturbation of an integrable system whose phase space is foliated by
invariant tori. In the general case such a foliation by tori can have singularities on
some surface in the phase space. However, for generic two-frequency systems no
estimates are known for the averaging method when such a surface is crossed. The
case of a single-frequency system has been investigated thoroughly: [19], Chap. 3
(see also [16], Chap. 6, § 1.10), [54], and [55]. Some results on two-frequency systems
can be found in [54] and [56].
   3) Statistical properties of the dynamics. As we mentioned in § 12, capture into
resonance can reasonably be regarded as a random event. Each case of passage
through a resonance without capture results in a deviation of the exact trajectory
of the slow variables from their trajectory in the averaged system. This deviation
can reasonably be treated as a random scattering on the resonance (cf. [39]). The
main questions relating to the statistical independence of successive captures into
836                                 A. I. Neishtadt

resonance and of the results of successive passages through resonances in general
two-frequency systems are still open. However, the considerable success achieved in
the model problem in the description of a single-frequency system with frequency
that can vanish [35], opens ways to resolve many of these questions. In [35] the
reader can also find the statements of several open problems relating to this topic.
   The main time scales in the problem under consideration are times of order 1/ε,
1/ε3/2 , and 1/ε2 . Now we discuss problems concerned with the statistical properties
of the dynamics for these times.
   On an interval of time of length ∼ 1/ε only finitely many captures into resonance
can occur along a trajectory (their number is independent of ε). If the number n
of the slow variables is at least 2 and certain conditions of general position are
satisfied, then successive captures into resonance can presumably be treated as
independent random events. √ If this assumption holds, then, since the probability
of each capture has order ε , the probability of two or more captures on a single
trajectory has order ε. The proof of independence in the single-frequency case was
an intermediate result in [35] (in [57] it was shown in a model example that the
results of successive passages through resonances are independent).
    In a time ∼ 1/ε3/2 the actual motion can deviate from the solution of the aver-
aged system by a quantity of order 1 due to capture into resonance for a set of
initial data with measure of order 1. A corresponding example was constructed in
[58], and in [35] the asymptotic behaviour of the measure of the set of initial data
corresponding to motions with capture into resonance occurring in this period of
time was calculated for a certain class of single-frequency systems. For this period
of time the deviation that accumulates between the actual motion and the solu-
tion of the averaged system is of order 1 for a set of initial data with measure of
order 1, due to summation of the mean values of the jumps of the slow variables
at the resonances (their repulsion from the resonances, in the terminology of [35]).
Moreover, it was shown in [35] that in the exact system small jumps ∼ ε1/2 of the
slow variables at a resonance can be summed as independent random variables in
first approximation. Then for phase points passing through a resonance without
capture these jumps result (in first approximation) in a deterministic drift of the
slow variables across the trajectories of the averaged system, with the drift velocity
determined by the mean value of the jump of the slow variables at a resonance.
   It is reasonable to consider periods of time ∼ 1/ε2 , for example, in the case
when all the resonances are weak (have no oscillation regions), so that capture into
resonance is impossible. Then the mean values of the jumps of slow variables at
resonances also vanish [39]. For such periods of time, summation of the jumps of the
slow variables at resonances presumably results in a diffusion of the slow variables
by a quantity of order 1. Such a diffusion can be observed in calculations (see, for
instance, [45] or [50]), however, no rigorous results in this direction are known. No
examples of such a diffusion have been investigated analytically.
   B) Averaging in multifrequency systems. The applicability of the averaging
method to multifrequency systems follows from the general result on averaging in
systems involving fast and slow motions: under quite general assumptions the mean
error (over all initial data) of the averaging method in such systems approaches
zero as the typical ratio ε of the velocities of the slow and fast motions tends to
               Averaging, passage through resonances, and capture into resonance               837


zero [1], [59], [60].6 Under various assumptions about the properties of the frequency
vector ω(I), sharp upper estimates for the mean value of the error of the averaging
method over all initial data have been obtained in the case of multifrequency sys-
tems with rotating phases (1.1) [20], [61], [62]. For example,√if there are no fewer
slow variables than there are frequencies, then this error is O( ε ) for a typical sys-
tem. It follows from this bound that, apart √ from a set of initial data with measure
κ, the error of the averaging method is O( ε/κ), and this estimate is close to being
sharp under the assumptions made. (These assumptions involve only the frequency
vector ω(I).) It is possible that the estimates can be improved if certain conditions
relating to the general mutual position of the resonant surfaces and the vector field
of the averaged system are imposed (this is a possible interpretation of the question
asked in problem 1972-10 in [22]). Already for three-frequency systems we know
of no estimates of the type in Theorem 1, when for any ε we can fairly explicitly
distinguish between the initial data for which the averaging method works and the
data for which this method can give an error of order 1.
